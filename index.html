Prequal on Envoy: open-source probe-based load balancing for service meshes

Bas Mens · Kira Morozova · Lumi Heemstra · Mathijs Meuzelaar · Zhenghan Zhong
CSE2000 Software Project, Delft University of Technology — commissioned by Uber B.V.
Code: Envoy-Prequal/envoy-prequal (modules) · load-balancing-benchmarker (harness)

We built the first open-source implementation of Prequal — Google's probe-based load-balancing algorithm (NSDI '24) that ran YouTube [1] — as a pair of Rust dynamic modules for Envoy. Across ~750 benchmark runs and 1.1 billion requests on a heterogeneous Kubernetes testbed, it matches Envoy's strongest built-in policy on latency with zero external configuration, and beats it outright where information is fragmented: service meshes, where every client balances from a partial view.

1Background: Why load balancing is a tail-latency problem

At scale, user experience is set by the slowest few percent of requests: a request fanning out to 100 backends is hostage to the worst one. Operators defend their p99 with over-provisioning, because the balancer's routing decision is only as good as its signal — and the classic signals are poor. Round-robin and random are blind to replica state. Least-request (power-of-two-choices) counts in-flight requests but treats a fast machine and a slow one as equals — a real problem on the mixed hardware generations that large fleets accumulate, as Uber has documented [2]. Latency-feedback policies learn a replica's speed only from their own completed requests, so their picture is always one job behind.

2What Prequal is, and why it works

Prequal [1] replaces inference with asking. For every request the balancer serves, it also fires a couple of asynchronous probes at randomly chosen replicas, off the request path. Each replica answers with two numbers: its requests-in-flight (RIF) — an exact count, right now — and a recent-latency estimate conditioned on similar load. Answers land in a small probe pool (16 entries, aged out within ~a second, reused under a budget), and requests are routed by the paper's hot–cold lexicographic rule: replicas above a RIF quantile (e.g. top 60%) threshold are "hot" and avoided; among the cold ones, take the lowest estimated latency; if everything is hot, take the lowest RIF.

Four properties make it work. First, the signal is fresh by construction: RIF read at probe time exposes a saturated or dead replica immediately, instead of after a queue has already formed (the probe pool's age-out bounds how stale any decision input can be). Second, the two signals are complementary: latency captures how fast a machine is (which absorbs heterogeneity — a slow tier reads as high latency even when idle), while RIF captures how busy it is (which catches sub-second queue buildup that averaged latency misses). Third, the design resists herding: every balancer would love the fleet's fastest replica, but each one chooses only among the handful of replicas it happened to probe — different balancers hold different random samples, so their picks decorrelate — and the moment a favored replica's in-flight count climbs, the hot–cold rule marks it hot and steers new traffic away before a queue can form. Fourth, probing cost is decoupled from serving: probes are tiny header-only exchanges, sent in proportion to traffic but never below a fixed floor, so the overhead stays negligible while the information rate stays high — even a balancer that serves almost no requests keeps a fresh view of the fleet.

3What we built, and why it was hard

Two Rust shared objects against Envoy's new dynamic-modules ABI [3] — among the first custom load-balancing policies on that interface, inside the proxy that carries much of the industry's service-mesh traffic. libprequal.so is the balancer: an async probe engine off the hot path, a fixed-size lock-scoped probe pool with budgeted reuse and alternating "oldest probe"/"worst backend" eviction, and a RIF-quantile estimator that classifies replicas hot or cold at pick time. libprequal_responder.so is the probe answerer: an Envoy HTTP filter (it forwards no traffic) that keeps an exact in-flight count and a RIF-conditioned sliding-window latency estimate, and serves both as two response headers on GET /probe. That two-header contract is the whole interface — any server implementing it can join, so deployments can bring their own middleware. Installing it is deliberately boring: two .so paths and a JSON config in envoy.yaml. No proxy fork, no application changes.

That simplicity was hard-won, and the hard parts were systems work, not algorithm transcription. Where this code sits, a single panic on the selection path takes the whole proxy down with it, so selection is wrapped to degrade to random choice rather than crash; Envoy's multi-worker threading model had to be reconciled with one shared probe pool; and microsecond budgets punish naive optimization — more than once the answer came from thinking mechanically and statistically rather than reaching for the asymptotic analysis every CS student is taught. The probe responder's sample store is the clearest example: a concurrent data structure of our own design — a grow-only spine on stable element addresses, an atomic in-flight counter, and locking scoped to a single per-RIF bucket — with its statistical choices benchmarked rather than assumed. At one point, we even designed a double-layered lock free ring buffer that spines on time instead of RIF, and replacing the running latency median with a mean, but despite the contention-free writes and constant time reads, the measurements said the precision wasn't worth the sort. Verification matched the stakes: 99 unit tests, CI that denies warnings, and a bench server that background-watches for performance regression — ~750 runs / 1.14 B requests total, all driven by our open-source benchmarking harness.

Observability got the same systems treatment. Both modules inject a full metrics suite — 24 series in all: 17 from the balancer (probe outcomes and RTTs, hot/cold classification, evictions, pool state, selection latency...), 5 from the probe responder, 2 for CPU — directly into Envoy's admin stats endpoint, where Prometheus scrapes them and purpose-built Grafana dashboards plot them. Even that came with an ABI landmine: stat registration and flushes must run on an Envoy-owned thread — calling the stats ABI from one of the module's own threads segfaults the proxy — so metrics are accumulated locally and flushed only from Envoy's callbacks every 100ms.

Every claim needed backing in numbers, so evaluation ran on a testbed built to reproduce what Google measured on hundreds of YouTube servers: a 21-replica, CPU-pinned k3s fleet with three capacity tiers (1 : 1.5 : 2), open-loop load via Nighthawk [5] — closed-loop generators self-throttle and hide queue collapse — plus seeded CPU antagonists and pod-kill schedules via Chaos Mesh [6].

The in-flight counter alone can't tell a fast busy machine from a slow one, so the slow tier saturates first. This reproduces, on commodity Envoy, the pattern in the Prequal paper's own replica-selection study [1]: at 70–90% utilization, least-loaded selection's tail grows without bound while Prequal's hot–cold rule holds it nearly flat — their densest testbed comparison and the direct ancestor of the tables above.

Under harsher conditions — pod churn (replicas killed and restarted mid-run, so endpoints keep vanishing and rejoining cold) plus CPU interference (antagonist processes burning cycles on the replicas' nodes, to further make real capacity unpredictable) — the same ordering holds:

5The strongest opponent: peak EWMA

Envoy's peak EWMA policy [4] is the one baseline that pushed us. It is Prequal's closest relative: like Prequal, it scores each replica by combining a latency estimate with an in-flight count, and picks among sampled candidates. The difference is where the information comes from. Peak EWMA observes passively — every completed request updates an exponentially-weighted moving average biased toward recent worst-case latency; its in-flight count is the balancer's own bookkeeping. No probes, no responder, no extra traffic: strictly lower overhead than Prequal.

Behind a central proxy, that overhead advantage is nearly free of cost, because a central balancer's own traffic is a dense, fresh sample stream — thousands of completed requests per second amount to free probing, and its in-flight counter is global. Tuned peak EWMA matched us there in steady state, under CPU contention, under spikes, and — equipped with health checks — came near parity under replica kills (97 vs 99 ms p99.9, 0.008% vs 0.004% failed at 4,500 req/s). In one configuration it beat us outright: stacking active health checks on top of Prequal in a mesh made things worse (ejections fight the probe pool — 0.50% errors vs hygienic peak EWMA's 0.30%).

We analyzed and tested, and this is the conclusion we arrived at: passive observation has two structural weaknesses, and both are about information rather than tuning.

  1. Silence is indistinguishable from health. A dead replica stops producing samples — no completed requests means no EWMA updates, so the latency estimate freezes at whatever it was the moment the replica died. Worse, the signal inverts: as outstanding requests time out, the in-flight count drains toward zero, so the dead endpoint looks like the least loaded choice in the pool. The balancer keeps sending traffic into the void until an external subsystem (health checking, outlier detection) removes the endpoint — and how fast that happens depends entirely on how that companion subsystem is configured. The dependence is a cliff, not a slope:
    The configuration cliff At 4,500 req/s under failures + contention, peak EWMA's failed-request rate spans 1.305% (no health checking) → 0.934% (Envoy's documented defaults: 4 s health-check interval, eject ≤10% of hosts for 30 s) → 0.008% (tuned: 2 s, ≤50%, 10 s) → 0.004% (aggressively tuned) — a 233× spread across plausible configs, against Prequal's 0.004% with no external configuration at all. Failure detection is the balancer's own probe loop, not a companion subsystem someone must tune and keep tuned.
  2. Estimate freshness is coupled to traffic volume. Passive observation only learns from requests the balancer itself sends, so the sampling rate per replica is dictated by the traffic pattern, not by how fast the backend's state is changing. Behind a central proxy this is fine — the aggregate stream is dense. But in a sidecar mesh, each balancer sees only its own thin slice of traffic: a replica that receives few requests from this particular client yields stale estimates precisely when the balancer needs fresh information to decide whether to send more. Prequal decouples the two — probe rate is independent of request rate, so information stays fresh even where traffic is thin. Section 6 puts this weakness to the test in a mesh setting.

6Where the information runs out: service meshes

Modern meshes don't have one balancer with a global view — they have one per client. Each sidecar sees only its own traffic; with subsetting, each sees only a slice of the fleet. Uber's dynamic-subsetting work describes exactly this world: per-host proxies balancing on local counters plus an aggregate load report that is "not strictly consistent across the fleet, but in practice it's close enough" [7]. This is where the passive/active distinction from Section 5 stops being an overhead question and becomes a correctness one: a per-client latency estimate built from thin local traffic is starved and stale, while a probe is a query any client can make, fresh, regardless of how much traffic it sends.

To measure this we ran bare Prequal against peak EWMA armed with its documented companions (outlier detection + active health checks), with per-client balancers. Failures were injected by seeded kill schedules: kills arrive at random exponential intervals averaging 45 seconds, and each one hard-terminates a randomly chosen replica of the 15-pod mesh fleet with zero grace period; the Deployment controller replaces the pod, so replicas keep dying and reappearing rather than the fleet shrinking. A schedule is fully determined by its seed — when replicas die and which fleet slots are hit — and both balancers replay the identical sequence (same kill times, same victims), on top of the same seeded background of noisy-neighbor CPU contention. The three schedules shown span the severity range a fleet actually sees. Schedule A is the harsh end: ten kills in its first three minutes — five inside one 22-second burst — so new failures pile on before earlier victims have recovered. Schedule B is the gentle end: seven kills over six minutes, almost all isolated. Schedule C is moderate churn arriving late: a single kill in its first three minutes, then nine in the next three, including pairs landing one second apart. We report error rate and failure-episode p99.9 — tail latency over just the stretches after a kill, while the fleet is degraded and recovering; outside those stretches the two balancers' tails show no consistent gap. A fourth run replays schedule A at 3× duration (10-minute windows) to rule out small-sample noise from the short windows:

The pattern is consistent across the spectrum: on the gentler schedules (B, C) the margin shows up more in error rate (probes notice a dead replica within a round-trip; health checks notice at their interval), on the harsh one (A) more in the failure-episode tail.

The mechanism is cleanest under a different disturbance — fast-changing noisy neighbors, a seeded antagonist that cuts a replica's capacity to 30% in 4-second bursts, faster than a latency estimate can track. Behind a central proxy it's a tie: a global in-flight count compensates. Rather, in the mesh (where per-client in-flight counts are noisy):

The last mesh setting is scatter-gather traffic: each request fans out into 10 sub-requests and completes at the pace of the slowest, with every sub-request placed independently by its client's own balancer. One bad pick anywhere drags the whole request, which makes fan-out the hardest regime for per-client balancing. On clean load (no failures injected) the two balancers finish at parity: over 10-minute windows Prequal was slightly ahead on every percentile — 55 / 135 / 386 ms vs 58 / 143 / 432 ms (p50 / p99 / p99.9) — but the short-run replication leaned the other way, so we claim no edge, only that probing keeps up. The lesson from this test is operational: client-side probing shares the sidecar's CPU with serving, and parity holds provided the proxy is budgeted for probe bursts.

Section conclusion Once balancing decisions are made from partial views — per-client balancers, subsetting, fan-out — the deciding resource is information per balancer, and probing is the only design here that scales it independently of traffic share. Bare Prequal beat a fully-equipped peak EWMA on errors and failure-episode tails across the whole severity spectrum we tested, and the gap replicated at 3× duration. Since fragmented views are the norm in production meshes, not the exception, this is the deployment where Prequal earns its keep.

7What this means for operators

Central proxy, stable fleet: a well-tuned peak EWMA is a fine choice; Prequal buys you out of the tuning, not the physics. Service mesh, mixed hardware, churn, subsetting, fan-out: Prequal's probe model is structurally matched to the information each balancer actually has — and it drops into an existing Envoy deployment as two .so files and a JSON config, no health-check subsystem to calibrate. Modules, configs, demo, and the full benchmarking harness are open source under github.com/Envoy-Prequal (Apache-2.0).

Limitations: results are from one 21-replica CPU-bound testbed with synthetic (if carefully shaped) load; near-saturation comparisons are noisy and we only claim what replicated; probing shares the sidecar's CPU, which matters at high fan-out; and the mesh conclusions are where we'd most welcome independent replication.
